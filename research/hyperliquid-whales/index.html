How much of the whale short is a bet?

November 2026 · Data from 2026-11-01 02:10 to 2026-11-10 03:15 UTC

"Whales are short BTC" gets posted every week. I wanted to know how much of that short is a view on price. A wallet that shorts $50M of HYPE while staking $50M of HYPE has no view at all, yet the raw long/short count still files it as a short.

Live dashboard Code and full method

What I found

Two thirds of the large HYPE shorts are hedges. Wallets hold spot or staked HYPE against $479M of the $695M in whale-size HYPE shorts. They earn staking yield and carry no price risk. Take those out and the $1M+ accounts go from 52% short to 31%. The traders with real exposure lean long.
Market makers don't explain the BTC short. I removed market makers, vaults, relative-value books and on-chain hedges, and the $1M+ group moved from 62% to 67% short. Market makers hold more BTC long than short, so dropping them pushes the share up. The largest BTC shorts trade 1 to 4 times their account per month and hold no BTC on Hyperliquid. They could be betting on a drop, or hedging BTC held on an exchange or in an ETF. On-chain data can't tell those apart.
The biggest accounts drive the headline. Across every tracked wallet with a $50k+ BTC position, 52% of the notional is short. Among accounts above $1M it is 62%. At the top, ETH sits at 56% and SOL at 58%.
The big accounts shorted into the sell-off. All four coins fell over the nine days: BTC 3.9%, ETH 6.5%, SOL 10% and HYPE 16%. ETH went from 48% short to 56% among $1M+ accounts, and HYPE from 47% to 52%. BTC moved inside a 9-point band (55.5% to 64.7%). A falling price doesn't move the share by itself, because longs and shorts in the same coin reprice together, so these changes come from trading.
Wallets with a track record lean further short. Among directional accounts that made $1M or more before the last 30 days, 76% of BTC exposure is short, 68% of ETH and 85% of SOL. In HYPE they sit at 21%, even more long than everyone else. I keep the last 30 days out of the pick on purpose: a filter on recent profit just selects whoever sat on the right side of the latest move.

How I measured it

Finding the wallets

Hyperliquid has no endpoint that lists every position. I start from the public leaderboard (about 48k addresses) and add any address that trades $50k or more in a day on the live BTC, ETH, SOL and HYPE trade stream. In its first hour the trade stream found 33 accounts above $1M that the leaderboard never listed.

About 25k wallets are in the set. The ~275 accounts above $1M get polled every 15 minutes and drive the charts above. The rest rotate. Tracked positions cover 44–47% of open interest, depending on the coin.

Labelling them

Directionaleverything else

For each short I also check the wallet's spot and staked balance in the same asset. The covered part counts as hedged and comes off the directional short. Labels get stamped into each snapshot, so a wallet that changes behaviour next month doesn't rewrite this month's chart.

Do the cut-offs drive the result?

I picked the 100× and 0.3 thresholds by hand, because turnover has no natural gap between market makers and active traders. So I reran the numbers across 24 combinations, from 30× to no market-maker filter and from 0.2 to no relative-value filter:

No combination flips a conclusion. (Snapshot of 2026-11-10 evening, so the raw figures differ a little from the ones above.)

What this can't see

  • Hedges held off Hyperliquid. "Unhedged" means unhedged on Hyperliquid.
  • Old positions in wallets that are neither on the leaderboard nor trading. That gap is why coverage sits below half.
  • Who owns which wallet. One fund can run several addresses.

All of this comes from nine days in which every coin fell. Read it as a description of that sample, and don't trade on it.

What's next

The level of positioning says little. The open question is whether changes in it lead price. Since November 2026 the pipeline logs every sharp shift in the $1M+ group, every $10M+ trade by a large account, and the positioning of a cohort that has made money over time, stamped as it was known at that moment. Once enough of these events pile up I will test whether price follows them more often than chance, and publish the result here either way.

After nine days, one signal stands out. In the hour after a $1M+ account changed a position by $10M or more, price moved 56 bps in the direction of that change on average (31 events, p < 0.001 against random timestamps with the same mix of directions). Shifts in the group's short share and in the profitable cohort showed nothing. I don't count this as a finding yet: the sample covers one falling market, several trades often land at the same moment, and I tested twelve signal and horizon pairs. I'll rerun it once each signal has a few hundred events.
